In the film How to Train Your Dragon, a village spends years fighting the creatures raiding it, until someone realises the dragons were never the real enemy. The relationship with design on a lot of teams right now feels like those raids, and this talk is about what happens when you stop fighting.

Sometimes a designer hands you a half-working AI prototype instead of a spec, and the handoff is worse than a spec would have been. Other times there’s no designer within reach and you’re expected to make the screen work yourself by Thursday. Either way, it lands in your lap, half-formed. Or an over-specified project causes its own problems, pinning everything down so tightly there’s no room left to push back.

The easiest reaction is to treat it as a threat, to fight, or to grind through alone. I want to argue for a new option because the friction you’re feeling isn’t really coming from designers, any more than the raids were ever really about the dragons. It’s coming from upstream pressures creating the situation for you and design.

The thing that fixes it is Object-Oriented UX, which is closer to how you already work than to anything on a mood board. You name the objects in the system, their attributes, and how they relate, and you do it before anyone opens Figma Make or a prompt window. That habit lets you interrogate a designer’s work instead of nodding at it, feed an AI proper structure so it stops generating confident nonsense, and catch a broken model before it costs you a sprint or more. The same object model works whether you’re flying with a human designer or an AI tool.

I'll walk through a worked example: one vague feature request, taken step by step to an object model, a sharper conversation with design, and AI output that would survive code review. You spend all day thinking in objects. Design does too, and OOUX is where those two habits turn out to be the same habit.

You’ll leave with a way to get an object model out of a fuzzy feature request, questions that pull better work from a design partner (or expose gaps fast when the design decisions are yours), a repeatable method for giving AI the context it needs to produce buildable UI, and enough shared vocabulary to close the gap between what design means and what you ship.

## Caitlin Steele

Caitlin Steele has worked as a developer and led design on tools developers use every day, including Trello, Confluence, and Jira. She's a Certified OOUX Strategist and has keynoted SwiftCraft, with talks at Pragma and iOSKonf. She works on the human side of building software at Thoughtful Apes.
